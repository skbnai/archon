## Pattern 3: Regulatory & Compliance Deep-Dive

These questions test article-level regulatory knowledge and practical implementation.

**Q9:** "What does EU AI Act Annex III mean for a bank deploying AI in credit scoring?" Annex III lists High-Risk AI categories; Category 5(b) explicitly covers systems evaluating creditworthiness or credit scores. This triggers: Art. 9 (a documented, systematic, ongoing risk management system); Art. 10 (training data governance — bias analysis, representativeness); Art. 11 (technical documentation per Annex IV's 15 required elements); Art. 12 (automatic logging of decision events); Art. 13 (transparency to users about AI involvement); Art. 14 (meaningful human oversight, aligned with GDPR Art. 22); Art. 15 (accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity); Art. 43 (conformity assessment before market placement); Art. 49 (EU declaration of conformity); and Art. 51 (registration in the EU High-Risk AI database). A common follow-up asks whether GDPR Art. 22 and EU AI Act Art. 14 are duplicative — they're complementary: GDPR gives the data subject a right (not to be subject to solely automated decisions), while the AI Act imposes a supply-side obligation on the deployer (to maintain meaningful oversight); a bank must satisfy both simultaneously.

**Q10:** "Walk me through ISO 42001:2023 and how it differs from ISO 27001." ISO 42001 focuses on the AI Management System versus ISO 27001's Information Security Management System, sharing the same PDCA/HLS Clause 4-10 structure but differing in subject matter: 42001 covers AI system inventory, AI impact assessment, and AI-specific risk and objectives, where 27001 covers asset inventory and CIA-triad security risk; 42001's Annex A holds organizational and technical AI controls versus 27001's 93 security controls; both use the same accredited certification bodies, and 42001 can integrate with an existing 27001 management system (indeed often follows it). Key clauses a Principal Architect must know: 4.3 (scope definition — which AI systems are in scope), 6.1 (AI risk management planning), 8.4 (AI impact assessment), 8.5 (system design and development governance), 9.1 (performance monitoring — fairness, accuracy, transparency), and 10.2 (AI-specific incident management).

**Q11:** "SR 11-7 was written for traditional models. How do you apply it to LLM-based AI systems?" SR 11-7 (Fed model risk management guidance) has three pillars, each reinterpreted for LLMs. Model development: SR 11-7 requires documenting purpose, assumptions, and limitations — for LLMs this means training data, fine-tuning approach, intended-use boundaries, known failure modes, and performance benchmarks on domain-specific test sets, not just general benchmarks. Independent validation: SR 11-7 requires validation by parties separate from developers — for LLMs this means independent adversarial red-teaming, bias evaluation, and qualitative human evaluation alongside automated metrics. Governance: SR 11-7 requires ongoing monitoring and performance review — for LLMs this means embedding drift detection, prediction distribution shift monitoring, SHAP value tracking, and constitutional violation rate monitoring, with defined thresholds for automatic model quarantine. The key gap: SR 11-7 assumes fixed feature inputs and quantitative outputs, while LLMs have variable natural language inputs and outputs, so traditional numerical validation doesn't fully transfer — 2026 best practice is hybrid validation combining quantitative evidence (accuracy, fairness, calibration) with qualitative evidence (expert red-team, constitutional audit).

## Pattern 4: Behavioral and Leadership Questions

These appear at Principal level and above, testing judgment, influence, and organizational leadership.

**Q12:** "Tell me about a time you had to push back on a business leader who wanted to deploy an AI system faster than governance allowed. What happened?" Tests courage under pressure and political navigation. Strong signals: naming a specific governance requirement rather than invoking vague "governance principles"; offering a path forward (a faster track, reduced scope) rather than a flat veto; engaging the leader's underlying business goal, not just citing the compliance gap; and holding the requirement without capitulating. Red flags: capitulating without securing adequate mitigations; blocking with no alternative offered; and citing no specific requirement ("it just didn't feel right").

**Q13:** "How would you explain constitutional AI to a board with no technical background?" A strong answer uses a code-of-conduct analogy: most AI today is trained on data and asked to do a job, with no explicit rules it must never break; constitutional AI writes those rules down as an AI constitution and has the AI check every action against them before acting — if an action would violate the constitution, the AI refuses and flags it for human review, like an employee saying "I can't do that, it violates our code of conduct." The distinguishing property versus other safety approaches: the rules are explicit and auditable, so the board can read the constitution, understand what the AI is and isn't allowed to do, and audit whether it's actually following the rules — very different from trusting a black-box model was "trained safely." Common follow-ups: what happens if the constitution itself contains a wrong principle, and how do you handle an agent that's technically constitutional but still producing bad outcomes.

**Q14:** "You've been asked to reduce AI governance overhead by 30%. What would you cut first, and what would you never cut?" Tests judgment about essentials versus overhead. Never cut: kill switch testing (failure here means you can't stop a runaway agent); runtime constitutional enforcement (the governance baseline); the audit ledger (a regulatory obligation — decisions can't be reconstructed without it); the human review gate for adverse decisions (GDPR Art. 22, regulatory); and fairness monitoring for protected characteristics (legal exposure). Can streamline: merging overlapping governance committees for lower-risk decisions; automating currently-manual compliance reporting; tiering ARB review (lightweight for L0/L1, full for L3+); and a risk-based approach to AI Impact Assessment depth. The governing principle: governance efficiency comes from risk-tiering and automation, never from removing controls on high-risk systems.

**Q15:** "A peer architect disagrees with your constitutional AI design. They argue that policy guardrails (OPA/Cedar alone) are sufficient without a constitutional classifier. How do you handle this?" Tests technical confidence and constructive disagreement handling. Acknowledge the legitimate point: Cedar/OPA is excellent for explicit, rule-based constraints and faster/more interpretable than a neural classifier — for principles fully specifiable as logical rules (e.g. "never transfer over $X without dual authorization"), Cedar is the right tool. Then explain the gap: the classifier covers what Cedar cannot — principles requiring semantic understanding of natural language output, like "never be deceptive" or "always maintain dignity in customer interactions," which can't be expressed as a rule and require context and tone analysis. The right design is layered: Cedar for explicit rules (fast, deterministic, auditable), the classifier for semantic principles (necessary but slower, with common patterns pre-computed and cached). Propose a joint design review mapping every constitutional principle to explicit-rule, semantic-principle, or hybrid, and design accordingly.

**Q16:** "Your AI governance model has been running for 18 months. The CISO says AI security is insufficient. The CDO says data governance is overlapping with yours. The CRO says AI risk is not integrated with enterprise risk. How do you resolve all three at once?" Tests organizational complexity navigation. This is a maturity problem, not a turf war — the model was right for deployment, now needs to evolve for scale. Run a governance operating model review with CISO/CDO/CRO and their leads: map current ownership and overlaps, document the gaps falling between the cracks, and produce a revised RACI within 30 days. For the CISO: AI security and AI governance aren't the same but must coordinate — create a joint forum defining the boundary (governance decides what AI can do and who's accountable; security protects AI from attack). For the CDO: data governance and AI governance share classification and lineage — agree that data governance owns classification while AI governance consumes it and extends it with AI-specific lineage (training data → model → decision). For the CRO: AI risk shouldn't be a separate registry — map AI risk categories into the enterprise risk taxonomy as an operational risk sub-category with an agreed escalation path. The outcome: one integrated governance model, not three tracks crossing at the edges.
