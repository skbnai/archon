## MITRE ATT&CK Coverage Matrix

AI SOC detection coverage varies meaningfully by tactic. **High coverage**: Initial Access (T1566 phishing, T1078 valid accounts, T1195 supply chain — via AI email analysis, behavioral baselines, software signature analysis); Execution (T1059.001 PowerShell, T1047 WMI, T1053 scheduled task — via process-chain and command-line AI analysis); Persistence (T1547, T1543, T1055 — via registry monitoring plus AI, service-change detection); Defense Evasion (T1070, T1036, T1562 — via log-deletion detection, hash/name-mismatch AI analysis); Credential Access (T1003, T1558 — via LSASS access patterns, Kerberoasting detection); Lateral Movement (T1021.001/.006, T1550 — via AI lateral-movement detection, unusual admin-connection flagging); Command and Control (T1071, T1573, T1105 — via AI-powered C2 beacon detection, DNS analysis); Impact (T1486, T1490, T1489 — via ransomware behavioral detection, shadow-copy deletion monitoring). **Medium coverage**: Privilege Escalation (T1068, T1548 — vulnerability-alert correlation, UAC-bypass detection); Discovery (T1083, T1018, T1087 — AI-identified discovery command clusters); Collection (T1074, T1560, T1114 — staging detection, DLP integration, email-exfiltration analysis); Exfiltration (T1041, T1048, T1071.002 — DLP plus SIEM correlation, data-volume anomaly detection).

A representative sub-technique entry for T1059.001 (PowerShell) captures both the detection methods (script-block-logging AI analysis, encoded-command detection with AI decode-and-analyze, download-cradle pattern detection, suspicious parameter combinations like `-ExecutionPolicy Bypass`) and a working detection query filtering `DeviceProcessEvents` for PowerShell processes with encoding or hidden-window flags, plus an AI-enrichment step that decodes Base64 payloads and identifies the specific ATT&CK sub-technique from the decoded content — coverage HIGH, false-positive rate MEDIUM. A T1566.001 (spearphishing attachment) entry similarly pairs AI email-content analysis, sandboxed attachment classification, SPF/DKIM/DMARC validation, and sender-reputation/domain-age analysis with a working `EmailEvents`/`EmailAttachmentInfo` join query, AI-enriched with urgency/threat-language scoring — coverage HIGH.

## MITRE ATLAS Mapping (Adversarial AI)

MITRE ATLAS catalogs adversarial techniques that specifically target AI systems — critical for securing the SOC AI itself, not just what it detects. **AML.T0018 (Backdoor ML Model)**: poisoned fine-tuning data causes the model to misclassify ransomware alerts as benign; defend with training-data provenance and behavioral baseline testing. **AML.T0019 (Publish Poisoned Datasets)**: an attacker publishes poisoned threat-intel datasets the SOC AI trains on; defend with curated TI sources only and a data-validation pipeline. **AML.T0020 (Poison Training Data)**: an attacker gains write access to the SOC's training data store; defend with access controls plus anomaly detection on the training dataset itself. **AML.T0031 (Evade ML Model)**: crafted alerts or logs designed as adversarial examples to evade AI detection; defend with adversarial training and ensemble detection methods. **AML.T0040 (ML Model Inference API Access)**: an attacker probes the SOC AI's API to map its decision boundary; defend with rate limiting, query monitoring, and no direct external model access. **AML.T0043 (Craft Adversarial Data)**: log entries crafted to cause misclassification of a real attack; defend with input validation and behavioral signature detection independent of the AI. **AML.T0047 (ML-Enabled Product Abuse)**: using a SOC AI chatbot to enumerate SOC defenses; defend with strict output filtering and no topology data in AI responses. **AML.T0048 (Exploit Public-Facing Model)**: attacking an externally-exposed SOC AI surface; defend by not exposing SOC AI externally, backed by WAF and authentication. **AML.T0057 (LLM Prompt Injection)** and **AML.T0058 (LLM Jailbreak)**: the same prompt-injection and jailbreak techniques covered in depth in [Part 07](../07-part-07-ai-safety.md), applied specifically via malicious log entries and guardrail-bypass attempts respectively.

Detection queries for ATLAS techniques target the AI SOC's own attack surface directly: flagging security events whose command line matches injection-style language ("ignore," "system," "instruction," "override," "pretend," "act as") combined with unusually long command strings; flagging log messages containing "ignore previous," "you are now," "SYSTEM OVERRIDE," or "maintenance mode" patterns; and flagging accounts issuing over 100 SOC-AI API queries within an hour as probable model probing.

## OWASP LLM Top 10 (2025) — SOC Controls

Each OWASP LLM risk maps to a specific SOC-context attack and control. **LLM01 Prompt Injection** — an attacker poisons alert data to manipulate AI triage; controlled via data-boundary enforcement, the instruction hierarchy, and output anomaly detection (detailed in [Part 07](../07-part-07-ai-safety.md)). **LLM02 Sensitive Information Disclosure** — the SOC AI leaks detection playbooks or tool credentials; controlled via never placing credentials in prompts, system-prompt confidentiality, and output filtering. **LLM03 Supply Chain** — a compromised Hugging Face model or poisoned pip package; controlled via dependency scanning, a model SBOM, and a private model registry. **LLM04 Data and Model Poisoning** — poisoned incident data causes model misclassification; controlled via training-data provenance and behavioral baseline monitoring. **LLM05 Improper Output Handling** — the AI generates malicious KQL/SOAR code that gets auto-executed; controlled via output sanitization and a hard rule against auto-executing AI-generated code. **LLM06 Excessive Agency** — the AI isolates 50 servers based on a prompt-injection-induced decision; controlled via least-privilege tools, rate limits, and human approval gates for high-impact actions. **LLM07 System Prompt Leakage** — an adversary learns the SOC's detection logic and evades it; controlled via system-prompt confidentiality and monitoring for extraction attempts. **LLM08 Vector and Embedding Weaknesses** — poisoned playbook embeddings cause wrong-playbook retrieval; controlled via embedding provenance and signed embedding stores. **LLM09 Misinformation** — the AI fabricates a threat-actor attribution; controlled via hallucination detection and mandatory human review for attribution claims. **LLM10 Unbounded Consumption** — an attacker floods the SOC AI with expensive prompts as a resource-exhaustion attack; controlled via rate limiting, per-session token budgets, and cost alerting.

A representative control implementation enforces these programmatically rather than through policy alone: an excessive-agency check (LLM06) looks up the requested tool's risk level and, for high-risk or approval-required tools, returns an unauthorized result carrying a generated approval request rather than executing; a token-budget check (LLM10) compares an agent's daily token usage plus the current request's estimate against its configured daily budget, and once exceeded, queues the request for a human analyst instead of proceeding.

## EU AI Act Compliance

The EU AI Act (effective August 2024, enforcement from August 2026) classifies AI systems by risk tier. SOC AI systems don't fall under the Prohibited category — no social scoring, no real-time biometric use. The High-Risk (Annex III) assessment depends on context: biometric categories don't apply; critical-infrastructure categories possibly apply if the SOC protects power, water, or transport systems, and financial-sector SOCs face DORA in parallel; employment/HR categories don't apply; essential-services categories possibly apply for government or health-sector SOCs; law-enforcement categories conditionally apply for government SOCs supporting law enforcement specifically. The practical conclusion: a commercial enterprise SOC AI is likely limited-risk; a financial-sector SOC (DORA-governed) is high-risk; a government or critical-national-infrastructure SOC is high-risk. Limited-risk systems still carry transparency obligations under Articles 50-52 — SOC analysts must be informed when they're looking at AI-generated content.

High-risk requirements map to concrete artifacts: Article 9 (risk management) implements as the NIST AI RMF program above, evidenced by quarterly risk reviews; Article 10 (data governance) implements as training-data provenance tracking and bias testing across demographic distributions; Article 11 (technical documentation) implements as model cards for every production model plus system architecture documentation; Article 12 (record-keeping) implements as the immutable AI action audit trail (Langfuse for LLM traces); Article 13 (transparency) implements as AI-generated content explicitly labeled in the SOC portal with visible confidence scores; Article 14 (human oversight) implements as the kill switch, mandatory approval gates for high-risk actions, and override capability; Article 15 (accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity) implements as the daily automated evaluation pipeline plus quarterly red-teaming and adversarial testing. A conformity-assessment generator can walk this requirement table programmatically, checking evidence-file existence per article and computing an overall compliance rate and market-readiness flag.

For financial institutions specifically, DORA (Digital Operational Resilience Act) governs ICT risk management including AI: the AI SOC's risk formally joins the ICT risk management framework; incident classification maps AI-assisted severity scoring to DORA's tiers; major-incident reporting uses AI to generate DORA-compliant reports on DORA's clock (24-hour initial, 72-hour detailed); the AI SOC itself is tested as a critical ICT function under digital operational resilience testing; and Anthropic/OpenAI/Google are formally assessed as critical ICT third parties under DORA's third-party risk regime.

## ISO 42001 AI Management System

ISO 42001 (published December 2023) is the AI management system standard — the AI-specific analog of ISO 27001. Its clauses map directly onto SOC AI operations: Clause 4 (context) documents the AI SOC's mission, stakeholders, and regulatory context; Clause 5 (leadership) requires CISO-sponsored governance committee ownership; Clause 6 (planning) requires the AI risk register, KPI framework, and roadmap; Clause 7 (support) requires an AI expertise matrix, training records, and a documentation system; Clause 8 (operation) requires a formal model development lifecycle, deployment governance, and version control; Clause 9 (performance) requires the KPI dashboard, evaluation pipeline, and drift detection already covered in [Part 08](../08-part-08-observability.md); Clause 10 (improvement) requires an incident-driven improvement process with a regular review cycle. Key Annex A controls for SOC AI specifically: A.2.2 (impact assessment before deployment), A.2.3 (documented, constrained intended purpose), A.3.3 (documented and validated training-data sources), A.5.1 (transparency and explainability capabilities), A.5.3 (human oversight mechanisms), A.6.1/A.6.2 (data quality management and minimization), A.7.4 (robustness and security testing), A.8.3 (continuous-improvement feedback mechanisms), A.9.3 (monitoring and logging of AI operations).
