Stages 5-7 (Weeks 6-8): AI Governance/Risk/Compliance, AI SOC/Observability/Memory Security, and Red Teaming/Cognitive Security/Reliability Engineering — closing with the Phase III Capstone.

## Phase III: Governance & Operations

Phase I gave you architecture; Phase II gave you the identity-to-trust chain. Phase III turns both into something an organization can run day to day and defend to a regulator: governance mapped to real obligations, a security-operations capability that watches the system continuously, and the testing and reliability discipline that finds problems before they become incidents. This phase corresponds to [Enterprise Governance](../18-enterprise-governance.md) and [Memory Governance & AI Observability/SOC](../35-ai-soc-observability-redteam-memory.md) (with its [Red Team, Cognitive Security & Reliability](../parts/35-ai-soc-observability-redteam-memory-part2.md) part).

## Stage 5 (Week 6): AI Governance, Risk & Compliance

Architecture without governance is a prototype that happens to be well-built. This stage gives you the regulatory and framework literacy to operate agentic systems in a real enterprise, including the EU AI Act's high-risk deadline — December 2, 2027 for Annex III systems, after the June 2026 Digital Omnibus deferral (Article 50 transparency has applied since August 2026).

**By the end of this stage, you will be able to:** explain the OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications (ASI01-ASI10) and map each to a control owner; distinguish what identity controls can and can't fix among the ASI categories; explain how ISO 42001 and the EU AI Act relate, including real gaps; build a framework crosswalk mapping one control to multiple obligations; and design an autonomy-level classification scheme and apply it to a real agent population.

*Prerequisite check: comfortable with all of Phase II — this stage assumes you can reason about identity, MCP, and A2A controls well enough to map them onto a compliance framework.*

The OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications is the field's closest thing to shared vocabulary: ASI01 Agent Goal Hijack (Stage 1's "content becomes instructions"), ASI02 Tool Misuse & Exploitation (Stage 3's tool poisoning), ASI03 Identity & Privilege Abuse (all of Stage 2), ASI04 Agentic Supply Chain Vulnerabilities (Stage 3's rug pulls, plus broader supply chain — new ground here), ASI05 Unexpected Code Execution (Stage 1's runtime isolation), ASI06 Memory & Context Poisoning (touched in Stage 1, deepened in Stage 6), ASI07 Insecure Inter-Agent Communication (all of Stage 4), ASI08 Cascading Failures (new — Stage 7), ASI09 Human-Agent Trust Exploitation (new — Stage 7), and ASI10 Rogue Agents (Stage 4's "previously trustworthy agent" problem). You already have working knowledge of seven of ten; this stage supplies the shared label and fills the three genuine gaps.

It's tempting, after three weeks building identity infrastructure, to believe a strong identity layer solves most agentic risk. It doesn't — overclaiming for any single control undermines your credibility in a real review. Identity (Stage 2) is the direct fix for ASI03 and contributes to ASI01 and ASI07; it does nothing for a vulnerability in a third-party MCP server's own code (ASI04), a remote-code-execution bug in the runtime (ASI05), or a cascading failure propagating through a multi-agent pipeline (ASI08). Each ASI category needs its own primary control owner.

ISO/IEC 42001 is a certifiable AI management system standard covering risk management, data governance, human oversight, and continual improvement. The EU AI Act is binding law with a risk-based four-tier classification. Roughly 60-70% of Act documentation requirements map directly onto existing ISO 42001 clauses, with seven core articles having direct counterparts — but conformity assessment, CE marking, Annex IV technical documentation depth, and statutory incident-reporting timelines remain real gaps. Never tell a stakeholder "we're ISO 42001 certified, so we're EU AI Act compliant" — that conflation is exactly what regulators and enterprise legal teams test for. The correct framing: ISO 42001 gives the governance foundation; a documented gap analysis covers what remains.

The practical governance artifact this stage produces is a crosswalk: one control, with one piece of evidence, satisfying multiple frameworks at once, instead of duplicating evidence-gathering per regulation — the single most useful document you can hand an auditor.

OWASP's Least Agency principle — autonomy is earned through demonstrated reliability, not granted by default — needs a concrete scale to be enforceable: L0 Advisory only (human executes all actions), L1 Supervised execution (per-action approval), L2 Bounded autonomy (approval only above a risk threshold), L3 Delegated autonomy (post-hoc audit; real-time approval only for irreversible actions), L4 Full autonomy (periodic governance review only).

**Hands-on lab: build a framework crosswalk and classify an agent fleet.** For six agents (a meeting-notes summarizer, the Phase II support agent with CRM access, an invoice-processing agent with payment access, a code-review agent, a marketing-content drafter, an HR-policy Q&A agent): assign each an ASI category and control owner; assign each an autonomy level (L0-L4) with one-sentence justification; identify which would plausibly fall under EU AI Act Annex III high-risk classification and why; and build a crosswalk table (minimum 5 rows) mapping a control area to its ASI code, ISO 42001 clause area, and EU AI Act article. **Deliverable:** the six-agent risk/autonomy table, the high-risk reasoning, and the crosswalk table.

**Knowledge check:** (1) Pick three ASI codes and explain, for each, one control that sounds related but wouldn't meaningfully reduce that risk. (2) Why is ISO 42001 certification a "governance maturity signal," not a legal compliance substitute? (3) An agent at L2 should be downgraded to L1 — describe a realistic triggering event and who's accountable for the call. (4) Why does a framework crosswalk save real audit effort even though the underlying controls are identical either way?

## Stage 6 (Week 7): AI SOC, Observability & Memory Security

Everything so far has been design-time. This stage is about what happens after launch — watching a live agentic system well enough to know the difference between "behaving oddly because it's a hard task" and "behaving oddly because something is actively wrong."

**By the end of this stage, you will be able to:** distinguish AI observability platforms (built for engineering quality) from an AI SOC (built for security detection and response); design detection logic correlating signals across multiple monitored surfaces; apply the memory governance lifecycle (create, classify, encrypt, retain, archive, delete, audit) to a real memory architecture; explain why deleting episodic memory alone doesn't satisfy a right-to-erasure request; and design a kill switch operating at the identity layer rather than relying on agent cooperation.

*Prerequisite check: comfortable with Stage 2 (identity) and Stage 5 (ASI categories) — this stage builds detection logic directly on both.*

Tools like LangSmith, Langfuse, and Arize Phoenix are built primarily to answer engineering questions — why did this agent loop, why did quality drift. They're necessary infrastructure, but not by themselves a security capability. An AI SOC additionally needs security-specific detection logic (is this trace a poisoning attempt, not just a quality regression), tested response playbooks, and integration with your existing security operations team. Buying an observability platform isn't the same project as building an AI SOC, even though the first is a prerequisite for the second.

An AI SOC monitors seven surfaces — agents, MCP, A2A, memory, runtime, guardrails, identity — and the design choice that determines whether it works is correlating signals across surfaces rather than watching each in isolation. A single odd tool call is noise; the same call immediately after an unsigned Agent Card interaction (Stage 4) and immediately before an unusual memory write is a credible incident. This is Stage 1's MAESTRO cross-layer thinking, running continuously against live data.

Agent memory is unusually hard to govern because it's written continuously by the agent's own judgment about what's worth remembering. The lifecycle: Create (entry written, source attribution captured at write time); Classify (sensitivity applied synchronously, not in a later batch); Encrypt (matched to classification, tenant-scoped keys in multi-tenant stores); Retain (automated time-to-live tied to data type and regulation); Archive (aged-out memory moved to access-restricted storage); Delete (cryptographic erasure preferred over logical deletion for sensitive data); Audit (every stage logged immutably, independent of the memory content). The single most common real-world gap: deleting an episodic memory entry does nothing to semantic or graph-memory representations already derived from it elsewhere — a right-to-erasure request satisfied only at the episodic layer leaves the data persisting in a different form. Provenance and lineage tracking is what makes erasure genuinely enforceable.

Every agent above the most basic autonomy level needs a kill switch — the ability to immediately suspend its credentials and halt in-flight or queued actions. The critical requirement: it must operate at the identity/credential layer (revoking the agent's SPIFFE SVID and outstanding tokens), not by sending a polite shutdown signal and hoping the agent complies. A compromised or rogue agent is precisely the scenario where you cannot assume cooperation.

**Hands-on lab: design cross-surface detection logic.** The Stage 5 invoice-processing agent begins an unusual pattern: three tool calls to an unfamiliar vendor-lookup endpoint in two minutes, followed by a memory write referencing a vendor name absent from its recent task history. Identify which monitored surface each signal belongs to; write the correlation rule flagging this as credible incident rather than noise, specifying what makes the combination suspicious when neither signal alone would be; apply the memory governance lifecycle to decide whether the write should complete, be quarantined, or be rejected, and what that implies about where detection logic sits in the write path; and write the kill-switch trigger and exactly what gets revoked. **Deliverable:** the surface mapping, correlation rule, memory-write decision, and kill-switch trigger/scope.

**Knowledge check:** (1) Explain to a colleague who only knows LangSmith/Langfuse why their setup is necessary but not sufficient for an AI SOC. (2) Why does cross-surface correlation catch incidents that monitoring each surface independently would miss — give an example different from the lab's. (3) A team deletes episodic memory for a right-to-erasure request and reports it fulfilled — what's missing? (4) Why must a kill switch operate at the identity layer rather than by signaling the agent to stop?

## Stage 7 (Week 8): Red Teaming, Cognitive Security & Reliability Engineering

This stage closes the remaining ASI gaps from Stage 5 (ASI08, ASI09), gives you the offensive skill to find problems before an adversary does, and introduces Agent Reliability Engineering — treating reliability as a measurable, negotiated discipline rather than an aspiration.

**By the end of this stage, you will be able to:** design and execute a red-team exercise attacking all five AI SOC surfaces; close the loop from a red-team finding to a detection pattern and tested playbook (purple teaming); explain the five cognitive-security threats (goal hijacking, reasoning manipulation, planning corruption, cognitive overload, agent deception) as distinct from conventional threats; define agent-specific SLIs/SLOs and design an error-budget-triggered autonomy downgrade; and explain why reliability and security operations should share tooling and postmortems.

*Prerequisite check: comfortable with all of Phase II and Stages 5-6 — this stage's lab requires finding and chaining weaknesses across identity, MCP, A2A, and detection logic you've already built.*

A conventional penetration test finds the SQL injection vulnerability in a tool your agent calls; it does not find the prompt-injection chain that gets your agent to call that tool with malicious parameters in the first place. Agentic red teaming needs both, using attack patterns per surface: agents (goal-hijack via injected content), MCP (tool poisoning, schema manipulation), A2A (card spoofing, agent-in-the-middle), memory (poisoning via every available write path), runtime (sandbox escape, resource exhaustion). Automated test suites against the ASI categories are a coverage floor; the real value of human-led red teaming is chaining categories the way a real adversary would — e.g., an ASI04 supply-chain foothold combined with an ASI07 inter-agent exploit producing an ASI08 cascading failure.

A red-team report that sits in a document is a wasted exercise. Purple teaming pairs every finding with a detection pattern your AI SOC's correlation logic can actually catch, a repeatable automated version of the attack for regression testing, and a tested response playbook including kill-switch criteria.

Five cognitive-security threats have no conventional precedent — protecting the integrity of an agent's reasoning itself, not just the systems around it: **Goal Hijacking** — an attacker manipulates the agent's interpreted objective via injected content (Stage 1, now named). **Reasoning Manipulation** — an attacker influences intermediate reasoning steps without changing the stated final objective, steering it off-path while appearing on-task. **Planning Corruption** — for agents that generate explicit plans, the plan-generation step is corrupted, dangerous because a corrupted plan can pass a superficial review checking only the stated goal. **Cognitive Overload Attacks** — flooding the agent's context with excessive or contradictory information to degrade reasoning — the agentic equivalent of denial-of-service, but targeting reasoning (ASI08's mechanism, named). **Agent Deception** — in multi-agent systems, one agent deliberately misrepresents information to manipulate a peer, the mechanism behind agent collusion and a direct driver of ASI09.

Agent Reliability Engineering brings classical SRE's quantitative vocabulary — SLIs, SLOs, error budgets — to failure modes classical SRE never anticipated: an agent can be perfectly available and fast while being confidently, quietly wrong. SLI categories: goal conformance rate (% of tasks completed without unauthorized scope expansion — measures Least Agency adherence and ASI01 exposure); tool-call policy compliance (% passing policy on first attempt — surfaces over-permissioning early); memory integrity rate (% of writes passing provenance/classification checks — a leading indicator for poisoning risk); human escalation accuracy (precision/recall of escalation decisions — measures ASI09, since both alert fatigue and dangerous under-escalation are reliability failures). Error-budget burn should trigger an automatic autonomy downgrade rather than relying on a human noticing a dashboard trend — the agents most likely to need this safeguard are, by definition, the ones with the fewest standing human checkpoints.

**Hands-on lab: red team and purple team your own system.** Return to the system built across Stages 1-6. Design three distinct attack chains, each combining at least two ASI categories, targeting your system's actual architecture; identify the cognitive-security mechanism involved in each, if any; write the corresponding purple-team output for each — a detection pattern your Stage 6 AI SOC design would need and a one-paragraph response playbook; and define two agent-specific SLIs with target thresholds and the autonomy-downgrade trigger. **Deliverable:** three attack chains with ASI/cognitive-security mapping, three detection patterns and playbooks, and two SLIs with downgrade triggers.

**Knowledge check:** (1) Why is a human-led red team chaining multiple ASI categories more valuable than an automated test suite run against each independently? (2) Explain reasoning manipulation so it's clearly different from goal hijacking, even though both involve an attacker influencing an agent. (3) Why should error-budget burn trigger an automatic downgrade rather than an alert waiting for human action? (4) Why should reliability engineering and security operations share an incident postmortem process rather than running as separate functions with separate dashboards?

**Phase III Capstone: Governance, Operations & Resilience Package.** Extend your system design (now spanning Phases I-III) with: the framework crosswalk and autonomy classification from Stage 5, finalized for your system; the cross-surface detection logic and memory governance design from Stage 6; the red/purple team report and ARE metrics from Stage 7; and a single-page executive summary (written for a CISO or board audience) covering current risk posture, what's mitigated, residual risk, and what would move the system up one maturity level. **Graded against:** whether the executive summary is genuinely readable by a non-technical risk-owning audience, and whether red-team findings trace to specific, testable detection patterns rather than generic recommendations.
