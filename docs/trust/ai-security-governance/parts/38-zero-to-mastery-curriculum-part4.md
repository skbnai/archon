## Phase IV: Economics & Mastery

The final phase covers agentic security's part that fits neither traditional cybersecurity nor traditional financial controls — agents that spend money and consume budget at machine speed — then closes the program with an integrated capstone requiring everything from Stages 0-8 at once. This phase corresponds to [Economic Security: FinOps, Commerce & Post-Quantum](../36-economic-security-finops-commerce-pqc.md) and [Enterprise Agent Operating Model & Maturity Model](../37-operating-model-maturity-roadmap.md).

## Stage 8 (Week 9): Economic Security — FinOps, Autonomous Commerce & Post-Quantum

This stage covers a category most security curricula skip: an agent's ability to spend, both compute budget and increasingly real money, faster than any human approval workflow was built to catch — plus a multi-year migration, post-quantum cryptography, that your identity infrastructure needs to anticipate now.

**By the end of this stage, you will be able to:** explain budget exhaustion and cost amplification attacks as simultaneously reliability and financial incidents; design a unified circuit breaker triggered by either security policy violation or budget breach; explain how AP2 (Agent Payments Protocol) makes payment intent cryptographically verifiable, and what it doesn't protect against; apply Stage 7's cognitive-security lesson to a payment-agent scenario; and explain why crypto-agility, not any single algorithm choice, is the binding constraint on post-quantum readiness.

*Prerequisite check: comfortable with Stage 4 (A2A trust) and Stage 7 (cognitive security) — this stage applies both to financial transactions.*

A misconfigured or manipulated agent in an infinite tool-call loop isn't just a reliability problem — it's a financial incident that can unfold in minutes rather than the days a traditional procurement-fraud control was designed to catch. **Budget Exhaustion Attacks** deliberately drive an agent or fleet to consume its allocated budget, denying service or acting as a direct denial-of-wallet attack; an unauthenticated A2A endpoint is a direct enabler, since it can be assigned unlimited resource-heavy tasks with no human approval gate. **Cost Amplification Attacks** exploit a multi-agent delegation chain so a single low-cost trigger fans out into many expensive downstream calls — the financial expression of ASI08's cascading-failure mechanism from Stage 7.

The design principle: build a single kill-switch and circuit-breaker mechanism triggerable by either security policy violation or budget-envelope breach, rather than two separate emergency-stop systems owned by two teams. Runaway cost is frequently the first observable symptom of a cascading failure or compromised agent, well before a security-specific signal fires — treating cost and security anomaly detection as the same investigation workflow catches incidents earlier.

Google's Agent Payments Protocol (AP2), now governed via the FIDO Alliance's Payments Technical Working Group, solves a problem traditional payment APIs weren't built for: they assume real-time human authorization at the moment of transaction, which doesn't hold when an agent acts on delegated authority, potentially asynchronously, on a task a human approved only in general terms earlier. AP2's core concepts: the Intent Mandate (what the user authorized in general terms — "book me a flight under $500"); the Cart Mandate (the specific transaction the agent assembled, cryptographically signed before execution); role separation (no single role — agent, merchant, processor — sees more than it needs); and verifiable settlement receipts (deterministic, auditable proof the transaction settled as authorized). Independent research reports a meaningful fraud-rate improvement under AP2's verifiable-intent model versus conventional API-based agent payment flows, concentrated in tampering-related fraud, since mandate signing makes tampering cryptographically detectable rather than merely procedurally discouraged.

This is the most important conceptual link in the stage: a verifiable-payment protocol secures the transaction, not the decision to transact. Published red-team research against AP2 confirms exactly what Stage 7's cognitive-security framework predicts — cryptographic guarantees protect a mandate's integrity once signed, but do nothing to prevent an agent from being manipulated, via the same goal-hijacking and reasoning-manipulation mechanisms, into forming and signing a mandate it should never have formed. The cryptography is sound; the upstream risk is unchanged. A payment agent needs everything from Stages 1-7 just as much as any other agent — AP2 adds a powerful additional layer, it doesn't replace the earlier ones.

The identity and trust infrastructure from Stage 2 (SPIFFE SVIDs), Stage 4 (signed Agent Cards), and this stage's AP2 mandates is exactly the long-lived, high-value cryptographic material "harvest-now-decrypt-later" attacks target: an adversary recording today's signed exchanges, intending to forge or decrypt them once cryptographically relevant quantum computers exist, is a realistic threat model for infrastructure expected to remain in service for years. FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) covers key exchange and is already deployed in hybrid configurations in production browsers and CDNs; FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) covers digital signatures and is the primary candidate for signing agent identity credentials and payment mandates going forward; FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) covers hash-based signatures as a conservative fallback — much larger signatures, use as a crypto-agility fallback, not a primary scheme.

Post-quantum migration is a multi-year project, not a 2026 deliverable, and shouldn't compete for budget against the active, currently-exploited risks addressed in Phases II and III. What you should do now: ensure every new identity, MCP, A2A, and payment-mandate component supports algorithm rotation — crypto-agility — without a hard-coded dependency baked into firmware or an unupgradable protocol version. That property turns the eventual PQC migration into a configuration change instead of a forklift replacement.

**Hands-on lab: design a payment-capable agent's economic controls.** The Stage 5 invoice-processing agent is being extended to autonomously approve and pay vendor invoices under $2,000 using AP2, with anything above routed to human approval. Design the per-task spend cap and the FinOps budget envelope as two independent, redundant controls, explaining what each catches that the other might miss; write the unified circuit-breaker trigger covering both a security-policy violation (an unrecognized vendor) and a budget-velocity anomaly (10 invoices in 60 seconds); describe a plausible prompt-injection scenario (e.g., via a malicious invoice PDF) causing the agent to form a fraudulent but cryptographically valid Cart Mandate, and identify the primary Stage 7 defense; and state your crypto-agility requirement for whatever signs this agent's mandates. **Deliverable:** the dual-control design, the circuit-breaker trigger, the injection-scenario analysis, and the crypto-agility statement.

**Knowledge check:** (1) Why should a budget-breach circuit breaker and a security-policy circuit breaker be the same mechanism rather than two separate systems? (2) Explain to someone who thinks "we use AP2, so our payment agent is secure" what AP2 actually guarantees and what it leaves unaddressed. (3) Why is crypto-agility "the binding constraint" on post-quantum readiness, rather than the specific choice of ML-KEM vs. ML-DSA vs. SLH-DSA? (4) Why is a payment-capable agent not a fundamentally different security problem from any other agent in this program — just one where failure's consequences are more immediately financial?

## Stage 9 (Week 10): Operating Model, Maturity & the Mastery Capstone

This final stage has two parts: the organizational layer that makes everything you've built operable by a real team — who owns what, how maturity is measured, how work gets prioritized — and the Mastery Capstone, an integrated exercise requiring everything from Stages 0-8 applied to one realistic scenario, structured the way a Principal Architect's actual first ninety days would be.

**By the end of this stage, you will be able to:** explain how the five operating-model functions divide accountability without creating silos; self-assess an organization against the five-level maturity model and identify a realistic next-level target; produce a complete, integrated architecture, governance package, and operating plan for a novel scenario under time constraint; and articulate, in interview-ready form, your reasoning on the program's core architectural tradeoffs.

*Prerequisite check: all of Stages 0-8 — this stage is primarily synthesis and assessment, introducing no new conceptual material.*

No single team owns agentic AI security end to end. The model mirrors how mature organizations already split traditional cybersecurity, cloud platform engineering, and data governance, distributing accountability across five functions: the **AI Governance Board** sets policy, approves autonomy thresholds, owns the framework crosswalk, and is the final escalation point for risk-acceptance decisions; the **AI Security Office** owns the Stage 1-4 architecture and policy — identity substrate, MCP/A2A gateways, governance fabric; the **Agent Operations Team** handles day-to-day lifecycle management (registration, autonomy changes, retirement), operationalizing the Agent Registry; the **Platform Engineering Team** builds and runs the runtime, gateways, and mesh infrastructure; the **AI SOC** provides continuous monitoring and incident response and operates the kill switch; and the **Red Team** conducts adversarial testing, kept organizationally independent to preserve objectivity. If you're an individual learner rather than part of an existing team, treat this as the org chart you'd propose on day one of a new role, not material to memorize for its own sake.

The five-level maturity model is for honest self-assessment, not a marketing artifact — most organizations deploying agents in production today, even well-resourced ones, sit at Level 1 or the early part of Level 2. **Level 1 (Ad Hoc):** static API keys and shared service accounts; no central registry or autonomy-level concept. **Level 2 (Aware):** a mix of static credentials and early workload-identity pilots; a registry exists but is incomplete, reviewed manually and periodically. **Level 3 (Managed):** ephemeral, workload-identity-issued credentials as default; the framework crosswalk operates and the Governance Board meets regularly. **Level 4 (Measured):** trust-score-informed dynamic authorization layered on identity; the purple-team loop is closed and KPIs are reported quarterly with trend data. **Level 5 (Optimizing):** hybrid classical/PQC credentials and verifiable-credential external trust; governance anticipates regulatory change and the organization is a reference case others benchmark against.

**The Mastery Capstone: Ninety-Day Plan for a New Agentic Platform.** This is the assessment the entire program builds toward — open-ended in scenario, specific in requirements, the same balance a real Principal Architect role strikes between "figure out what matters here" and "deliver something concrete by Friday." You've just joined an organization (choose a realistic industry — financial services, healthcare, retail, or your own choosing) as its first Enterprise Agentic AI Security Architect. It already runs roughly 40 AI agents in production, deployed by individual teams with no central oversight, no consistent identity model, and no governance function. Leadership has given you ninety days to produce an assessment and a plan.

**Requirements:** Architecture & Threat Modeling (Stages 0-1) — a MAESTRO-based threat model for at least one representative agent, including a cross-layer threat and a runtime isolation decision. Identity, MCP & A2A Design (Stages 2-4) — a target-state compound identity architecture, an MCP gateway design, and an A2A trust-broker design, written as a migration plan from the organization's current ad hoc state, not a greenfield design. Governance & Compliance (Stage 5) — a framework crosswalk and autonomy-level classification applied to a representative slice of the portfolio, plus an explicit call on EU AI Act high-risk classification. Operations & Resilience (Stages 6-7) — an AI SOC design (seven surfaces, at least one cross-surface correlation rule), a memory governance application, and a 90-day red-team plan with at least two attack chains. Economic Security (Stage 8) — a FinOps/circuit-breaker design if the portfolio plausibly includes cost or payment exposure, or a justification plus forward-looking design for the most likely next payment-capable use case. Operating Model & Maturity (Stage 9) — a proposed org structure adapted to the organization's size, a maturity self-assessment (starting at Level 1), and a realistic 12-month target with the three highest-priority workstreams. Executive Summary — a two-page summary for the CEO and board covering current risk posture, the plan, and the resources (headcount, budget, timeline) requested.

**Graded against:** whether the plan is genuinely sequenced and prioritized — does it identify the identity substrate as the highest-leverage early investment, consistent with this program's central lesson, rather than treating all sections as equally urgent in parallel; technical correctness within each section against the Stages 0-8 standards; whether the executive summary is actually readable and persuasive to a nontechnical, risk-owning audience; and realism — does the plan reflect what a real organization with finite budget and people could execute, or read as an unprioritized wish list.

**Recommended time allocation:** research and scenario definition, 0.5 day; architecture and threat modeling, 1 day; identity/MCP/A2A design, 1.5 days; governance and compliance, 0.5 day; operations and resilience, 1 day; economic security, 0.5 day; operating model, maturity, and executive summary, 1 day.

## After the Capstone: Where to Go Next

Completing this curriculum gets a learner to working competence — able to threat-model, design, govern, and operate without supervision on a bounded, realistic scenario. It does not, by itself, produce the scar tissue of having lived through real incidents, vendor negotiations, and regulatory audits. That gap closes through practice, not further reading.

Three concrete next steps: pursue a credential that validates this knowledge externally — CAISP (Certified AI Security Professional) is the broadest single AI security certification as of 2026 and maps closely to this curriculum's balance of offense, defense, and governance, and the [Principal Architect Interview Guide](../parts/37-operating-model-maturity-roadmap-part2.md) is a useful self-test beforehand. Apply this curriculum's pattern — threat model, identity design, gateway design, governance crosswalk, operational plan — to a real system at your own organization, even a small one, rather than only this document's fictional scenarios; a real system will surprise you in ways a curriculum cannot. And track the standards landscape directly going forward, not just this curriculum's snapshot of it — NIST's AI Agent Standards Initiative, the IETF WIMSE working group, the OWASP MCP Top 10, and the FIDO Alliance's AP2 post-quantum profile were all explicitly still in motion when this program was written; re-verify the current state of each before making a significant architecture investment based on them.
