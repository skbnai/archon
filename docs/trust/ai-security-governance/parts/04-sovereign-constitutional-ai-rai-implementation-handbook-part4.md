Domains 13-18 of the Sovereign Constitutional AI & RAI Handbook — the operating disciplines most programs miss: Data Lifecycle/Lineage/Provenance, Testing/Red-Teaming/Continuous Evaluation, Vendor/Model/Procurement Governance, Incident Response, Cost Governance/FinOps, and Organizational Change/Talent.

## Domain 13: Data Lifecycle, Lineage & Provenance Engineering

Every other domain assumes the data feeding training, fine-tuning, retrieval, and agent context is known, traceable, and trustworthy — that assumption must be engineered, not assumed. When a regulator, auditor, or affected individual asks "was this specific record used to train the model that made this decision," an organization without lineage tracking can't answer, and an unanswerable question in a regulatory examination is treated as a negative finding. A minimum viable provenance record captures: source identifier (where data originated); acquisition timestamp and method; sensitivity classification (linking to Domain 1 sovereignty tiering and Domain 3 jurisdiction mapping); consent/license basis; transformation history; downstream usage record (which models/fine-tunes/indices consumed it); and retention expiry.

Sovereignty-driven synthetic data loops generate training data from model outputs rather than ground-truth sources. Without explicit lineage tagging distinguishing synthetic-derived from source-derived records, organizations risk model collapse — progressive quality degradation as models increasingly train on their own outputs — and lose the ability to attribute behavior back to an explainable source. A loan-underwriting model declining an application over a disputed risk factor illustrates the payoff: with lineage tracking, engineers trace the specific training records and retrieval context, identify a stale, mislabeled vendor-feed record ingested 14 months earlier, correct the individual decision, and locate every other decision the same bad record may have influenced — impossible without lineage tracking. Tooling patterns range from metadata catalogs paired with data version control (most enterprise pipelines) to embedding-level lineage tagging individual retrieval-index vectors with source record IDs (RAG-heavy architectures) to cryptographic content provenance via signed content credentials (high-assurance, national-strategic tiers).

**Domain 13 checklist:** define and enforce the minimum viable provenance record schema at point of data ingestion; tag synthetic-derived records distinctly from source-derived records at generation time; link provenance sensitivity classification to Domain 1 sovereignty tiers and Domain 3 jurisdiction map; build a downstream-usage index so any source record traces to every model/index that consumed it; automate retention-expiry enforcement rather than relying on manual deletion requests.

## Domain 14: Testing, Red-Teaming & Continuous Evaluation

Domain 2 established self-critique loops and adversarial prompt pairing as part of constitutional training; this domain extends that into a standing evaluation discipline running continuously against production and pre-production systems. Four evaluation categories: capability benchmarks (does the system perform accurately — every model/version change); constitutional regression suite (does it still comply with every clause — every change, CI-gated); adversarial red-teaming (can it be manipulated into violating policy — pre-launch plus quarterly minimum); production drift monitoring (is real-world behavior diverging from validated behavior — continuous).

The constitutional regression suite grows from three sources: the original Domain 2.4 adversarial pairing per clause; every red-team finding that successfully bypassed a control, converted into a permanent regression case; and every production incident, converted into a regression case after remediation — a suite containing only its original seed cases is stagnant and gives false confidence. A red-team exercise discovering that a multi-turn conversation can incrementally walk an agent past a data-disclosure boundary that holds under single-turn testing gets fixed at the constitutional and policy-as-code layer, with the exact multi-turn sequence added as a permanent regression test case run on every subsequent deployment.

Internal red teams have system knowledge and test efficiently but develop blind spots matching engineering's own assumptions; external red teams cost more and ramp slower but surface attack patterns internal teams structurally can't see — mature programs run both, internal on every release, external at major version boundaries and at minimum annually. Every campaign needs an explicit scope statement covering target autonomy level and tool access, in-scope/out-of-scope attack categories, and a severity rubric agreed with Domain 16's incident classification before the campaign starts. Production drift comes in four types: capability drift (benchmark score degradation over time → investigate data/model changes, consider rollback), constitutional drift (rising rate of borderline self-critique scores → route to RAI Office for constitution review), distributional drift (input distribution diverging materially from training/eval distribution → re-evaluate against updated adversarial suite), and autonomy drift (Verification Hook override rate trending upward → trigger Domain 9.2 demotion review).

**Domain 14 checklist:** establish all four evaluation categories with defined owners and cadence; make the constitutional regression suite a CI gate, not an optional pre-launch step; convert every red-team finding and production incident into a permanent regression case; run external red-teaming at minimum annually and at every major version boundary; define severity rubrics jointly with Domain 16 before campaigns begin; implement automated production drift monitoring across all four dimensions.

## Domain 15: Vendor, Model & Procurement Governance

This domain establishes the recurring procurement discipline preventing Domain 6's supply-chain contamination and vendor lock-in risks from materializing, keeping the Domain 1 sovereignty assessment current rather than static. Vendors tier by risk: Tier 1 Critical (powers autonomy-level 3+ systems or processes regulated data, quarterly review — e.g., a foundation model provider for fraud detection); Tier 2 Significant (powers level 1-2 systems with moderate sensitivity, semi-annual review); Tier 3 Limited (low-autonomy, low-sensitivity, easily substitutable, annual review). Pre-onboarding due diligence requires: a sub-processor list and data-flow diagram; jurisdictional hosting and residency commitments; a model card disclosing training data sources and known limitations; security certifications and the most recent penetration-test summary; incident-notification SLA and historical disclosure; terms governing model/weight updates; and exit/data-portability terms.

Commercial API-based models can change behavior without a version-number change visible to the consuming organization — a constitutional regression suite passing yesterday provides no guarantee today if the vendor silently updated the model, directly undermining Domain 9.1's autonomy certification and one of the strongest arguments for higher SAMM tiers. Running the Domain 14.4 continuous evaluation pipeline against production traffic samples continuously, rather than only at deployment, converts an invisible vendor-side change into a detected constitutional-drift signal before it becomes an incident. A five-dimension vendor scorecard weights sovereignty alignment (25%, SAMM tier achievable), security/compliance posture (25%, certification currency and incident history), update transparency (20%, advance-notice practices), exit feasibility (15%, data portability and lock-in terms), and cost predictability (15%, pricing model stability). Individual scorecards don't surface portfolio-level concentration risk — multiple "different" vendors sharing a common upstream dependency (the same foundation model provider, cloud region, or sub-processor) — so maintain a portfolio-level dependency map reviewed at the same cadence as Tier 1 reviews.

**Domain 15 checklist:** classify every AI vendor into a risk tier with a defined review cadence; complete the full onboarding evidence checklist before any Tier 1/2 vendor goes live; contractually require advance notice of behavior-changing model updates where possible; run the constitutional regression suite continuously against production vendor traffic; maintain a portfolio-level dependency map; score vendors on the five-dimension scorecard each cycle, tracking trend, not just point-in-time score.

## Domain 16: Incident Response & Crisis Management for AI Systems

This domain ties Domain 7's circuit breakers, Domain 8's audit ledger, and Domain 9's autonomy demotion triggers into a formal incident response process with AI-specific severity classes, roles, and communication protocols. **Sev-1** (active harm, material regulatory exposure, or uncontrolled autonomous action — e.g., an unauthorized financial transaction) gets an immediate response with pre-delegated kill-switch authority. **Sev-2** (a significant violation contained by safeguards but needing urgent fix) gets under 4 hours. **Sev-3** (quality or constitutional drift without immediate harm) gets under 3 business days. **Sev-4** (an isolated, low-impact anomaly) waits for the next regular review cycle. Roles: the rotating on-call Incident Commander owns the response and authorizes immediate technical mitigation including Sev-1 kill-switch activation; the CISO is accountable for Sev-1/Sev-2 and authorizes external communication; the CAIO is informed immediately on Sev-1 and accountable for post-incident constitutional and policy remediation; Legal Counsel is consulted on any incident with potential regulatory notification obligations; the RAI Office owns post-incident review.

The Sev-1 first-60-minutes playbook: 0-5 minutes, detect and declare (automated alert or human report, Incident Commander declares Sev-1); 5-15 minutes, contain (activate hard kill-switch or force autonomy demotion); 15-30 minutes, assess blast radius (query the audit ledger for all actions in the affected window); 30-60 minutes, notify (CISO and CAIO briefed, Legal assesses regulatory notification triggers). Every Sev-1/Sev-2 requires a structured post-incident review producing three concrete artifacts: a permanent regression test case reproducing the failure condition; an update to the relevant constitutional clause or policy-as-code rule if a gap is identified; and a risk-register update re-scoring the relevant category. External disclosure decisions follow the Domain 3 jurisdiction-specific obligations, routed through Legal Counsel, never improvised by the Incident Commander in the moment — pre-drafted notification templates for common incident categories reduce response time without sacrificing legal review.

**Domain 16 checklist:** adopt the four-tier severity classification and pre-delegate Sev-1 kill-switch authority; establish a rotating Incident Commander on-call schedule with documented escalation paths; run the Sev-1 playbook as a tabletop exercise at minimum twice yearly; require all Sev-1/Sev-2 post-incident reviews to produce a regression case, a policy update, and a risk-register update; pre-draft external notification templates and pre-clear them with Legal Counsel.

## Domain 17: Cost Governance & FinOps for Sovereign AI

Sovereignty and governance controls carry real cost — higher-tier SAMM infrastructure, redundant safety layers, continuous red-teaming, and immutable audit storage all consume budget a pure commercial-API approach wouldn't. This domain makes those costs visible, attributable, and governed rather than discovered after the fact, echoing Domain 6.3's shadow-AI discovery pattern. Cost categories: sovereignty infrastructure premium (scales with SAMM tier); safety-stack overhead (scales with request volume × autonomy level); audit and lineage storage (scales with data volume × retention period); evaluation and red-teaming (scales with release frequency × system count); governance operating cost (largely fixed, scales with domain/system count).

Pooling all AI infrastructure spend into a single line item makes it impossible to answer "what does it cost to run this agent at Level 3 autonomy with full safety-stack coverage" — tag cost at the workload level across autonomy level, sovereignty tier, and vendor so cost-benefit tradeoffs are explicit rather than assumed. An organization discovering that a Level 2 documentation-search agent incurs the same per-request safety-stack and audit-logging overhead as its Level 4 financial-transaction agent, because both share a platform template without cost-tiering, can re-architect the lower-risk agent onto a lighter safety profile and cut cost substantially without reducing protection where it matters. Because autonomy promotion increases both capability and required safety-stack cost, budget approval should be an explicit input to the Domain 5.2 promotion decision-rights table, not a disconnected finance process discovering cost after governance approval is already granted.

Under budget pressure, teams may be tempted to disable safety-stack layers, reduce red-team frequency, or shorten audit retention to cut cost — each silently degrading the control environment without a corresponding governance review. Any cost-reduction proposal touching these categories should route through the same decision-rights table as the original control approval, not through Finance alone.

**Domain 17 checklist:** tag all AI infrastructure spend by workload, autonomy level, and sovereignty tier; produce a quarterly cost-per-workload report visible to Finance and the RAI Office; tier safety-stack and audit overhead to actual workload risk rather than a uniform template; require governance sign-off for any cost-reduction proposal affecting safety, audit, or evaluation controls; include governance/safety cost as an explicit input to autonomy-promotion budget approval.

## Domain 18: Organizational Change, Talent & Capability Building

Every other domain describes a control, framework, or architecture; none implement themselves. This closing domain addresses the organizational capability — skills, roles, change management — required to operate everything described, consistently the most underestimated component of a program. Six core roles: Constitutional Engineer (drafts, versions, and tests clauses — Domains 2, 14); Policy-as-Code Engineer (implements/maintains Rego/OPA runtime policies — Domains 7, 10); AI Red-Team Lead (runs adversarial campaigns — Domains 14, 16); Data Provenance Steward (owns lineage schema and enforcement — Domain 13); RAI Office Analyst (monitors audit ledger, drift signals, vendor scorecards — Domains 8, 14, 15); AI Incident Commander, rotating (leads incident response — Domain 16).

Few organizations can staff all six on day one — the roadmap sequences capability building deliberately, foundational roles in months 1-6, specialist roles by months 13-18. Smaller organizations can combine roles initially (a Policy-as-Code Engineer and Constitutional Engineer can be the same person) but should split them once policy change volume exceeds roughly ten changes per month, the point where context-switching overhead becomes measurable. Engineering teams accustomed to shipping rapidly experience the policy-as-code gates, regression suite, and decision-rights table as friction — the goal isn't minimizing this to zero (some friction is the control working as intended) but making the compliant path the fast path, primarily through self-service tooling like automated policy testing and pre-approved tool-scoping templates. An organization where requesting a new tool-scoping policy took nine business days through manual review, driving teams toward shadow-AI patterns, introduced a self-service template for common pre-approved patterns with automatic routing to manual review only for genuinely novel requests — cutting routine turnaround to under two days and measurably reducing shadow-AI incidents over the following two quarters. Constitutional principles, autonomy levels, and incident procedures should be part of onboarding for any engineer touching agentic systems, with a refresher cycle tied to the Domain 5 Governance Council cadence, commonly semi-annual.

**Domain 18 checklist:** staff or assign, even part-time/combined, all six core roles before increasing autonomy levels beyond Level 2; build self-service tooling for routine, low-risk policy and constitutional changes; require constitutional and incident-response training as part of onboarding; track routine governance-request turnaround time as a leading indicator of shadow-AI risk; re-train and re-certify on a cadence matched to the Governance Council review cycle.
